Decision note · Department of Social Justice & Empowerment

How Citizens Find Schemes

The Department publishes 141 schemes. A citizen who does not already know a scheme by name has no reliable way to reach the ones meant for them. This note sets out the options designed to fix that, and asks for three decisions.

Prepared for Ministry review Site dosje.gov.in (SAMAVESH) Date August 2026 Status For decision
Section 01

What we are asking for

Three decisions, independent of one another. Each can be taken on its own; none blocks the others.

D1
Decision one · the homepage

Which entry point does the homepage carry? Option A keeps and repairs the persona panel that is already there. Option B adds a short guided section that asks a few questions and returns a shortlist.

Our recommendation: adopt Option B, and repair Option A rather than remove it. The two serve different visitors.

D2
Decision two · the schemes page

How is the scheme list filtered and presented? Option A places a row of persona pictures above a grid of scheme cards. Option B places a target-group panel down the left and lists schemes as a table.

Our recommendation: Option B, because this is the page on which people compare schemes, and a table can carry who runs it and whether it is central or state.

D3
Decision three · the assistant

Does Samajik Sahayak, the assistant already on the site, also carry the questions? It would ask the same questions one at a time inside the chat panel.

Our recommendation: yes, but after the website work — it draws on the same scheme records, and building it first would mean building those records twice.

Section 02

Why this is being proposed

The site already has a filter for schemes. It works. The information behind it does not.

Every scheme published on dosje.gov.in can be tagged with the group it serves, and the schemes page offers that tag as a filter. When we examined the Department’s own published records, the tagging was found to be largely absent:

Group the Department servesSchemes that address themSchemes tagged so the filter finds them
Scheduled Castes325
Other Backward Classes and EBC246
De-notified, nomadic and semi-nomadic communities4314
Women and girls230
Voluntary organisations120
Victims of caste atrocities30
Persons affected by drugs or alcohol30
Transgender persons20

Two further facts about the same records. Twenty-seven of the 141 schemes carry no group tag at all, and can therefore be reached only by someone who already knows the web address. And only two of the 141 are tagged for more than one group — so a Scheduled Caste student must choose between filtering by community, which returns five schemes and not one scholarship among them, or by student, which returns fifty-one with no community narrowing. The two cannot be combined into a truthful answer.

In plain terms

A young woman from a Scheduled Caste family, in class 11, looking for help with fees, is served by several schemes of this Department. On the site as it stands today, there is no sequence of clicks that reliably brings her to them.

This is also a compliance matter. GIGW 3.0, the Government of India’s guidelines for government websites, states that its key thrust is “the implementation of user-centric information architecture to ease the user journey and provide content as per the user profile”. Its checkpoint 21 additionally requires integration with the national scheme-discovery platform, myScheme. Neither is met today.

Section 03 · Mode 1, the website

The homepage

Decision D1. What the visitor meets first, before they have read anything.

Option A

The persona panel that already exists

The blue “Explore User Personas” panel now on the homepage, beside Recent Documents. It shows one illustrated role at a time — Student, Government Official — with arrows to move between them, under the line “Choose your role to discover services made for you.”

What changes

Nothing visually. What changes is what the panel does: choosing a role would take the visitor to the schemes page already filtered to that group. Today it does not do this, and the promise in its own subtitle goes unmet.

Strengths

  • Already designed, already built, already on the page — the cheapest of every option here.
  • The illustrations carry warmth and help visitors who read slowly or read little English.
  • Recognising yourself is easier than describing yourself. Some visitors will never answer a question but will point at a picture.
  • Adds no height to a homepage that already runs to roughly 7,280 pixels.

Limitations

  • Shows one role at a time behind arrows, so the visitor cannot see whether they are represented without clicking through.
  • Only two roles exist today, and one of them is “Beneficiary” — the Department’s word for everybody, not a group anyone recognises themselves as.
  • It narrows by group and nothing else. It cannot account for stage of life, the kind of help needed, or the State the person lives in.
  • Someone who belongs to two groups has to pick one door and lose the other.
Best suited to visitors who return, who identify strongly with one group, and who already have some idea of what the Department offers.
Build effort · low Added page height · none Needs group tagging on every scheme
Option B

A short guided section that returns a shortlist

A new section headed “Find support for you”, asking up to five short questions — who the help is for, which group describes you, stage of life, the kind of help needed, and the State — then showing the schemes that match, each with what you get, who runs it, and what you will need.

What the visitor sees

One question on screen at a time, with a running count of how many schemes still match — the design shows “45” falling as answers are given. Every question can be skipped, and skipping widens the answer rather than stopping it. The result is a list headed “What you can apply for”, each entry ending in a real place to apply.

Strengths

  • Accounts for more than group: stage of life, kind of help, and State together, which is how entitlement actually works.
  • Nobody is asked to sign in, and no personal information is collected or stored.
  • The falling count is honest feedback — the visitor can see the answer narrowing rather than trusting a black box.
  • Names the boundary of the Department: choosing “Person with Disability” routes immediately and plainly to the Department of Empowerment of Persons with Disabilities, instead of returning nothing.
  • Answers a question no other option answers: what am I actually entitled to look at?

Limitations

  • Adds roughly 150 pixels of height to the homepage, before results are shown.
  • Asks about caste on the second question. The wording, and the reason given for asking, need the Department’s explicit approval.
  • Requires every scheme to be tagged on four axes before it works at all — the largest single piece of preparatory work in this note.
  • A visitor who abandons the questions halfway has been given nothing, unless the design keeps a visible way out at every step. It does, but this must be preserved in build.
Best suited to first-time visitors, and anyone who does not know a scheme by name — which, on the evidence of the site’s own search records, is most people.
Build effort · medium Added page height · ~150px Needs full four-axis tagging
Recommendation on D1

Adopt Option B, and repair Option A rather than remove it. They are not competitors. Option A serves the visitor who recognises themselves in a picture; Option B serves the one who cannot, and does not know where to start. Option A costs almost nothing to repair, and the repair is required in any case, because a panel that promises to “discover services made for you” and then does not is worse than no panel at all.

Section 04 · Mode 1, the website

The schemes page

Decision D2. Both options filter by target group. They differ in how the results are shown, and that difference matters more than it looks.

Option A

Persona strip above a grid of scheme cards

A row of nine illustrated groups across the top — Marginalised Individuals, Scheduled Caste, DNT and Nomadic Tribes, Safai Karamchari, Senior Citizen, Transgender Person, Drug Rehabilitation, Student, NGO or Organisation. Below it, “Active Schemes” as three-across picture cards.

Strengths

  • All nine groups are visible at once. No arrows, no scrolling to find yourself.
  • The illustrations do real work for visitors with limited literacy, and in a country of many languages a picture travels further than a label.
  • Scheme cards carry an image, which makes the page feel welcoming rather than administrative.
  • Consistent with how the homepage already presents personas, so the visitor meets the same idea twice.

Limitations

  • A card grid fits far fewer schemes per screen. With 141 schemes this means heavy scrolling or heavy pagination.
  • There is little room on a card for the facts that decide whether a person can apply — who runs it, whether it is central or from a particular State.
  • Comparing two schemes means holding both in memory, because they cannot be seen side by side in a common format.
  • Nine illustrations must be drawn, approved and maintained, and every new group needs another.
Best suited to browsing — a visitor who wants to see what exists for their group, without a specific scheme in mind.
Build effort · medium Nine illustrations to produce Fewer schemes visible per screen
Option B

Target-group panel with a scheme table

The same nine groups as a “Target Group” panel down the left, alongside stage of life and kind of help. Results appear as a table: the scheme, what you get, who runs it, and whether it is central, State or through a corporation.

Strengths

  • Filters combine. A Scheduled Caste applicant of working age looking for credit can express all three at once, which Option A cannot do.
  • Carries jurisdiction on every row. Today, State schemes sit in the same list as central ones with nothing to distinguish them, and a citizen cannot tell whether they may apply.
  • Far more schemes visible per screen, and directly comparable because every row is in the same format.
  • A filtered view can be given its own web address, so an officer or a helpline can send a citizen a link that opens already narrowed.
  • Shows a count against each filter, so a combination that would return nothing is visible before it is chosen.

Limitations

  • A table is plainer. It reads as an official record rather than an invitation.
  • Asks the citizen to express their situation in the site’s vocabulary, which is a harder demand than pointing at a picture.
  • Needs more care to work well on a small screen, where a left panel has to become something else.
  • Loses the illustrations unless they are retained in the group panel.
Best suited to deciding — and to officers, voluntary organisations, helpline staff and journalists, for whom this page is a working tool.
Build effort · medium No new illustrations required Shareable filtered links
Recommendation on D2

Option B. The schemes page is where a citizen decides, and deciding needs facts that a picture card has no room for — above all whether a scheme is central or belongs to another State. If the Department wishes to keep the illustrations, the group panel in Option B can carry the same nine images at the top as a quick-choice row, and still keep the table beneath. That is a small addition, not a separate option.

Section 05 · Mode 2

The assistant

Decision D3. Samajik Sahayak is already on the site and already offers “Which scheme applies to me?” — and then answers in general terms that narrow nothing.

Mode 2

The same questions, asked one at a time in conversation

The assistant asks the five questions as chat messages with tappable answers, reports the count after each one (“That leaves 8”), and ends by naming the schemes that list the person as their target group, with a way to open each.

The rule it works under

The assistant does not rule on entitlement. In the design it says: “8 schemes list your organisation as their target group. Whether an application succeeds is the sanctioning authority’s decision, not mine.” This is deliberate and must not be softened in build. The assistant routes; it does not decide.

Strengths

  • Reachable from every page, including a page that was not found and a search that returned nothing — the only option here that can rescue a dead end.
  • One question per screen suits a mobile telephone, which is how most citizens will arrive.
  • Can hold the same conversation in Hindi and other languages without a second page design.
  • Already built and already approved for the estate; only the scheme branch is new work.

Limitations

  • It cannot be discovered. A small button in the corner is found by people already looking for help; it does nothing for a visitor scrolling past.
  • On a small screen the panel covers the page beneath it.
  • A conversation is harder for screen-reader users to review than a page, so the same content must exist on the website regardless.
  • If it is built with its own private copy of the scheme records, the Department ends up maintaining two catalogues that will drift apart.
Best suited to mobile visitors, people who have hit a dead end, and anyone more comfortable being asked than filling in a form. It is a further way in to the same records — not a substitute for the website work.
Build effort · low, once the records exist Component already in service Must not be built first
Recommendation on D3

Yes, and after the website work. The assistant draws on exactly the same scheme records as the homepage section and the schemes page. Built afterwards it is a small addition. Built first it forces those records to be created twice, in two places, with no guarantee they agree.

Section 07

The one thing every option depends on

Whatever is decided, the same preparatory work is required first, and it is not a design task.

Each of the 141 schemes must carry structured information about who it is for. Not a paragraph of prose — a set of recorded values, and more than one where more than one applies:

Recorded against every schemeValues
Community or situationScheduled Caste · OBC or EBC · DNT, nomadic or semi-nomadic · sanitation and waste workers · women and girls · senior citizens · transgender persons · persons in destitution · persons affected by drugs · victims of atrocities · voluntary organisations
Stage of lifeIn school · in college or beyond · working age · senior citizen · any
Kind of helpEducation and fees · money to work with · a home · health and care · safety and legal help · skills and a job
JurisdictionCentral · a named State · through a corporation
How to applyThe portal, or the office, and the documents required
The point that decides everything else

A scheme must be findable under every value that applies to it, at once. A scholarship for Scheduled Caste girls in class 11 has to appear under Scheduled Caste, under women and girls, under school students and under education — simultaneously. Today only two schemes out of 141 carry more than one tag, which is precisely why the existing filter cannot give a truthful answer. Every option in this note is a way of looking at this information. None of them can work before it exists.

Section 08

Safeguards built into all of the options

Five commitments that hold whichever decisions are taken.

Nothing here decides a case

No screen and no message states that a person is eligible. The wording throughout is that a scheme lists the person as its target group, and that the sanctioning authority decides. This distinction is not cosmetic: a citizen who acts on a wrong assurance from a government website bears a real cost, and the Department would bear the complaint.

The boundary of the Department is stated, not hidden

Persons with disabilities are served by the Department of Empowerment of Persons with Disabilities, a separate department of this Ministry. Anyone who indicates a disability is told so plainly and directed to depwd.gov.in, rather than being shown an empty result. A confident redirection reads as competence; a blank page reads as a broken website.

No personal information is collected

No sign-in, no Aadhaar, no telephone number, no name, no income figure. Community and stage of life are chosen from fixed options and are never typed or stored. The questions exist to narrow a public list, nothing more.

Leaving the site is always announced

Many schemes are applied for elsewhere — the National Scholarship Portal, e-Anudaan, a State corporation. No button hands a citizen to another site silently. One screen first names where they are going, what will be asked for there, and offers a way back. Where a scheme has no online route at all, that is said plainly, with the office and the papers to carry.

Accessibility and standards

All screens are to meet WCAG 2.2 AA and the Government of India’s GIGW 3.0 guidelines, including keyboard operation, visible focus and screen-reader order. Because a conversation is harder to review with a screen reader than a page, every answer the assistant can give also exists on the website itself.

Section 11

Two corrections needed before this is circulated

Both were found in the design file itself. Neither is a design fault, but both would mislead a reader.

Correction one · the notes beside the designs are from another exercise

Every “Strengths and Limitations” block in the Service Discovery frame has been carried over from an earlier design exercise about a login screen. They refer to portals, to Citizen and Admin roles, to labels reading “Benefits & helpline” and “Approvals & MIS”, and to a split-screen sign-in layout. One states that “Option B is the recommended direction” for reasons that have nothing to do with finding a scheme.

None of it describes what is drawn above it. Sections 03 to 05 of this note replace those blocks. The design file should be updated to match before it is shown to anyone outside the project team, or the notes removed from the frame.

Correction two · the designs disagree about DEPwD

On the schemes page, the scheme cards include entries for persons with disabilities, marked as run by DEPwD. In the homepage questions, choosing “Person with Disability” is described as “Handled by a different department” and routes the citizen away.

Both are defensible; they cannot both be built. Either this is a catalogue of the whole Ministry, in which case the routing message is wrong and DEPwD must be a partner in the tagging, or it is a catalogue of this Department, in which case the DEPwD cards should not appear. This is a decision for the Ministry, and it is listed in Section 09.
